{{< lead >}}
How do the location decisions of multinational companies shape the network of cities in a region, and can we see it in the data?
{{< /lead >}}

{{< facts >}}

## The challenge

The Vales Region is home to two of Brazil's most globalized agro-industrial chains: **tobacco** in the Rio Pardo Valley and **poultry** in the Taquari Valley. Both are dominated by multinational companies, yet the two sub-regions developed very different urban networks. The research asked whether the organization of each production chain explains that difference.

## Approach

I mapped every establishment of the two chains and connected them to employment, export and corporate headquarters data, producing more than 30 original maps.

{{< step label="Firms" >}}

{{< /step >}}
{{< step label="Jobs & trade" >}}

{{< /step >}}
{{< step label="Flows" >}}

{{< /step >}}
{{< step label="Comparison" accent="true" >}}
Rio Pardo Valley vs. Taquari Valley urban networks
{{< /step >}}

## Results

- The **tobacco chain** is highly centralized: processing plants of multinational companies concentrate in Santa Cruz do Sul and Venâncio Aires, producing a **monocentric**, simpler urban network in the Rio Pardo Valley. Employment is also strongly seasonal, with hiring peaks in February–March and layoffs in August–September.
- The **poultry chain** is spread across many municipalities, with slaughterhouses and feed factories distributed through the Taquari Valley, producing a **polycentric** network with denser interactions between cities.
- The urban network is both a reflection of and a condition for the territorial division of labor.

## Why it matters

The findings show that regional development policies cannot treat neighboring regions as the same: the structure of the dominant economic activity shapes where jobs, services and infrastructure are needed.
